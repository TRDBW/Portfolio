Estimation

Implementing story points: a practical guide

15 May 2026 · 4 min read

Story points are the most used — and most misused — estimation tool in agile delivery. Done well, they give a team a fast, honest way to size work and forecast. Done badly, they become hours with extra steps. This is the standard method I use to introduce them.

What a story point actually measures

A story point is a relative measure of effort. It blends three things: the volume of work, its complexity, and the uncertainty or risk around it. It is deliberately not a unit of time — a 2 is roughly twice the effort of a 1, whoever picks it up. That relativity is what lets a team estimate quickly and consistently without debating whose hours count.

Use the modified Fibonacci scale

The industry standard is the modified Fibonacci sequence: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21. The widening gaps are the point — the bigger a piece of work, the less precision anyone really has, and the scale forces a meaningful choice between "about a 5" and "about an 8" instead of a fake debate between 6 and 7.

1–2 Trivial or small, well understood, no surprises expected.

3–5 A typical sprint-sized story — some complexity, manageable unknowns.

8 Large. Fine occasionally, but a strong candidate for splitting.

13+ Too big to commit to a sprint — split it before it enters one.

Anchor the scale with reference stories

Points only mean something relative to other work, so give the team anchors. Pick two or three recently completed, well-understood stories and agree their sizes as a group — a solid 2, a solid 5. Every new estimate is then a comparison ("bigger than the login fix, smaller than the reporting export") rather than a guess from nothing.

Estimate together with planning poker

The whole team estimates each story simultaneously — cards, fingers or a tool — and reveals at once, so nobody anchors on the loudest voice. When the numbers diverge, the outliers speak first: the person on 2 and the person on 13 usually know something the rest of the room doesn't. Discuss briefly, re-vote, converge. Two or three minutes per story is plenty; if it takes longer, the story isn't understood, and that discovery is the real value of the exercise.

If your points convert neatly to hours, you don't have story points — you have hours with a laundering step.

Let velocity emerge — don't set it

Velocity is the number of points a team completes per sprint, and it only exists in hindsight. After three or so sprints, the rolling average becomes a genuinely useful planning number: it tells you how much the team can take on and when a release will land. Resist the urge to target it, inflate it or "improve" it — the moment velocity becomes a goal, the estimates bend to serve it and the forecast dies.

The rules that keep points honest

  • Never convert points to hours — not in planning, not in reporting.
  • Never compare velocity across teams; each team's scale is its own.
  • Never use velocity as a performance metric — it's a forecasting input.
  • The people doing the work estimate it — not managers, not the loudest senior.
  • Re-estimate only when scope materially changes, not because work was hard.

Getting started: the first two sprints

Sprint zero: agree the scale, pick your reference stories, and poker the top of the backlog. Sprints one and two: track what actually gets done, and in retro compare the biggest estimate misses against reality — not to re-score them, but to sharpen the team's shared sense of size. By sprint three you'll have a usable velocity and, more importantly, a team that argues about scope instead of numbers.

Story points are a conversation tool. The number is a by-product; the shared understanding it forces is the deliverable.
