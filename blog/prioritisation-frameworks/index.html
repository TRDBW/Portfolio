Prioritisation

Agile prioritisation frameworks

5 Jun 2026 · 8 min read

When to use MoSCoW, RICE, WSJF and other decision-making tools.

Prioritisation frameworks can help teams compare options, expose assumptions and structure difficult conversations.

They cannot remove uncertainty or produce a universally correct answer.

Every framework emphasises particular information. One may favour reach, another may focus on delay, while another separates essential scope from optional scope.

The right method depends on the decision being made.

Simple stack ranking

Stack ranking places items in a single order from first to last.

The team compares the available options and decides which should come first. It continues until each item under consideration has a distinct position.

  • The number of items is manageable
  • The work is reasonably well understood
  • A clear delivery sequence is required
  • The team can explain its decision criteria

The order can become subjective when the reasoning is not visible.

It also becomes difficult to compare a very large number of unrelated items.

A team ranks:

  1. Resolve a production payment defect
  2. Complete a regulatory consent change
  3. Improve account search
  4. Add report filters

The value of stack ranking is that it creates an actual order rather than a collection of High-priority labels.

MoSCoW

MoSCoW groups scope into:

  • Must have
  • Should have
  • Could have
  • Won't have this time

It is particularly useful when a team must protect a minimum viable outcome within a fixed delivery window.

A Must should be essential for the release to remain viable, safe or compliant.

A Should is important but can be deferred.

A Could is desirable but not necessary.

A Won't-have item is explicitly outside the current scope.

  • A release has a fixed date
  • Scope must be negotiated
  • A minimum viable outcome needs to be protected
  • Stakeholders need clarity about exclusions

Teams often classify almost everything as a Must.

MoSCoW also does not create an exact order inside each category.

For a regulatory release:

  • Updated consent capture: Must
  • Internal compliance dashboard: Should
  • Advanced dashboard filters: Could
  • Historical data reclassification: Won't have this time

Value versus effort

Value-versus-effort analysis compares expected benefit with delivery investment.

Items are usually placed into four broad areas:

  • High value, low effort
  • High value, high effort
  • Low value, low effort
  • Low value, high effort
  • Comparing early options
  • Identifying potential quick wins
  • Challenging large, low-value proposals
  • Supporting a simple prioritisation workshop

It may ignore urgency, risk, strategic alignment and dependencies.

A high-value, high-effort item can still be more important than several easy wins.

An improved payment reminder may offer high value for relatively low effort. A complete billing portal may also offer high value but require substantially greater investment.

Delivering the reminder first does not automatically mean the larger capability should be abandoned.

RICE

RICE compares:

  • Reach
  • Impact
  • Confidence
  • Effort

The common formula is:

RICE score = Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort

Reach estimates how many people or events will be affected during a defined period.

Impact estimates how strongly the change will influence the intended outcome.

Confidence represents how reliable the team believes its estimates are.

Effort represents the expected delivery investment.

  • Product opportunities affect different numbers of customers
  • The team has evidence about likely reach
  • Several feature options need a consistent comparison

RICE can create false precision.

It may favour broad but shallow improvements over important work affecting a smaller group. It also does not naturally account for mandatory work or sequencing dependencies.

A checkout improvement affecting 10,000 customers may score above an administrative feature affecting 100 users.

That result may be reasonable, but a contractual or regulatory obligation could still change the final order.

Weighted Shortest Job First

Weighted Shortest Job First, or WSJF, compares the Cost of Delay with relative job size.

WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Size

Cost of Delay is often assessed using:

  • User or business value
  • Time criticality
  • Risk reduction or opportunity enablement

Job Size represents the relative delivery investment.

An item with a high Cost of Delay and a relatively small size receives a higher score.

  • Several substantial items compete for capacity
  • Delay has meaningful consequences
  • Relative size estimates are available
  • Teams need a structured economic comparison

The input scales can be subjective.

Teams may also manipulate scores to support a preferred result. Dependencies and mandatory sequencing still require separate judgement.

An unsupported security dependency may have strong time criticality and risk reduction with moderate effort. It may score above a larger visual redesign with little immediate Cost of Delay.

Cost of Delay

Cost of Delay estimates the value lost when work is delivered later instead of sooner.

The cost may involve:

  • Lost revenue
  • Increased operating cost
  • Continued risk exposure
  • Lost market opportunity
  • Customer dissatisfaction
  • Contractual consequences
  • Ongoing manual work

The estimate can be financial or relative.

  • Timing materially affects value
  • Seasonal opportunities exist
  • Regulatory or contractual dates apply
  • Several valuable items have different consequences of delay

The cost may be uncertain or difficult to isolate.

Teams should show assumptions rather than presenting speculative numbers as facts.

Delaying a seasonal booking capability by two months may remove most of its value for the year. Delaying an internal reporting enhancement by two months may have little measurable impact.

Kano Model

The Kano Model considers how capabilities influence customer satisfaction.

Typical categories include:

  • Must-be needs: Customers expect them, and their absence creates dissatisfaction.
  • Performance needs: Better performance creates greater satisfaction.
  • Delighters: Unexpected capabilities that create a positive response.
  • Indifferent features: Capabilities with little effect on satisfaction.
  • Exploring customer expectations
  • Balancing basic needs with differentiation
  • Conducting product discovery
  • Challenging assumptions about requested features

Customer expectations change.

A former delighter can become a basic expectation. The model also does not determine effort or technical sequence.

Real-time order tracking may once have been a delighter but may now be considered a basic expectation in a mature market.

Opportunity Scoring

Opportunity Scoring compares:

  • How important an outcome is to customers
  • How satisfied customers are with the current experience

High-importance, low-satisfaction outcomes represent stronger opportunities.

  • Identifying unmet customer needs
  • Comparing problem areas
  • Avoiding feature-led discovery
  • Prioritising research and product opportunities

The result depends on asking the right customers and describing outcomes clearly.

It identifies a problem worth addressing, not the correct solution.

Customers may rate "recover access to my account quickly" as highly important and current satisfaction as low.

That identifies a strong opportunity. It does not prove that a complete account-management portal is the correct response.

Which framework should a team use?

Teams do not need to use every method.

A framework is useful when it:

  • Makes assumptions visible
  • Encourages consistent criteria
  • Reveals disagreement
  • Supports transparent trade-offs
  • Can be explained to stakeholders

A framework becomes harmful when teams:

  • Treat a score as objective truth
  • Manipulate inputs
  • Ignore strategic context
  • Ignore dependencies
  • Preserve an outdated result
  • Spend more time scoring than learning

A prioritisation framework should improve the conversation.

It should never become a substitute for evidence and judgement.
