Agile frameworks compared: Scrum, Kanban and XP

17 Jul 2026 · 5 min read
Explore the interactive version, with live tools →

The three most common agile frameworks answer three different questions. What each one actually optimises for, how they truly differ, and why the strongest teams refuse to pick just one.

"Are you Scrum or Kanban?" is one of the most common — and least useful — questions in agile delivery. It treats a framework as a team's identity rather than what it is: a tool chosen to fit the shape of the work.

Scrum, Kanban and Extreme Programming (XP) are the three you will meet most often. They overlap, they disagree, and — importantly — they combine. Understanding what each is for is what lets you choose well, and mix deliberately.

Scrum manages complexity with cadence. Kanban manages flow with limits. XP manages quality with engineering discipline. Different problems, different tools.

The three at a glance

Before comparing them, it helps to see what each is built to do.

Scrum

A cadence for complex work

Fixed Sprints, three roles and a regular loop to plan, inspect and adapt. A predictable heartbeat for complex products.

Kanban

A system for flow

Continuous pull, explicit limits on work in progress, and no prescribed roles or iterations. Optimises how work flows.

XP

A discipline for quality

Engineering practices — TDD, pairing, CI — that keep changing software cheap and safe. Concerned with how you build.

Scrum: a protected rhythm

Scrum boxes work into fixed-length Sprints and wraps them in a small set of events and roles. The point is rhythm: a complex product gets a predictable cadence for planning, building, showing and improving.

Three roles carry it: a Product Owner who owns the ordered backlog, a Scrum Master who protects the process, and the Developers who build the increment. The trade-off is deliberate: Scrum resists change mid-Sprint. That is a strength when priorities are stable for a week or two, and a friction when they are not.

Kanban: optimise the flow

Kanban adds no roles and no iterations. It does three things: make the work visible, limit how much is in progress at once, and pull the next item only when there is capacity to start it. Limiting WIP is the move that matters — it forces you to finish before you start, and makes the bottleneck impossible to ignore.

Kanban shines when work is varied and unpredictable — support, operations, or a stream of small requests that would never fit neatly into a planned Sprint. There is no forced cadence, so re-prioritising costs nothing.

XP: the discipline of building well

Extreme Programming is less about how you manage work and more about how you build it. Where Scrum and Kanban largely stay silent on engineering, XP is explicit — and its practices are what most modern teams actually run, often without ever calling it XP.

Test-driven development

Write the failing test first; let it drive the design.

Pair programming

Two people, one problem — review built into the writing.

Continuous integration

Merge and verify constantly, so problems surface small.

Refactoring

Improve the design continuously, without changing behaviour.

Small releases

Ship in thin slices; shorten the feedback loop.

Simple design

Build the simplest thing that works; add complexity only when it earns its place.

Because XP is about the how, it layers cleanly on top of Scrum or Kanban rather than competing with them — which is exactly why the combinations below are so common.

They are not mutually exclusive

This is the part the "which one are we?" question misses entirely. Almost no strong team runs a single framework by the book. The two most common blends:

Scrumban

A Scrum planning cadence with Kanban WIP limits and pull. Common for teams balancing planned product work against a reactive stream.

Scrum + XP

Scrum for the what and when ; XP's TDD, pairing and CI for the how . The most durable pairing in practice.

Choosing well

Whatever you land on, the same three principles keep the choice honest:

  • Diagnose the work first. How does it arrive, how often does it change, and where does it actually break? The work chooses the framework, not the other way round.
  • Adopt practices, not identities. Take a Sprint cadence, a WIP limit and a pairing habit if they help — you do not owe any framework purity.
  • Measure the outcome, not the ceremony count. Flow, quality and predictability are the scoreboard; the number of meetings never was.

Where it goes wrong

Most framework failures are one of three patterns — and none is the framework's fault:

  • Cargo cult. Running the ceremonies without the mindset — stand-ups that only report, retros that change nothing.
  • Framework as identity. Defending the label once the work has outgrown it, rather than adapting the process to fit.
  • Skipping the engineering. Scrum or Kanban with no XP practices underneath — fast planning stacked on fragile code.

Conclusion

Scrum, Kanban and XP are not competing religions to convert to. They are tools built for different problems: cadence for complexity, flow for unpredictability, engineering discipline for quality. And when scale enters the picture, these same choices reappear one level up — which is where a framework like SAFe coordinates many teams at once.

Pick the framework the work needs — then borrow freely. The label is nothing; the outcome is everything.
