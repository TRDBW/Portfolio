Agile prioritisation: turning importance into an executable order

27 May 2026 · 6 min read

Why the most important item is not always the first item a team should deliver.

Prioritisation sounds simple until several genuinely important pieces of work compete for the same team, budget and delivery window.

One item may offer the greatest business value. Another may affect the largest number of customers. A third may be required before either can be delivered. There may also be a regulatory deadline, a production incident or an unsupported technical dependency that cannot be ignored.

All of these items can be important for different reasons.

The useful question is not simply:

Which item is most important?

Teams need to ask:

  • Which item creates the greatest customer or business outcome?
  • Which is most urgent?
  • Which reduces the greatest risk?
  • Which supports the product strategy?
  • Which unlocks other valuable work?
  • Which is sufficiently understood to deliver?
  • Which is the most realistic item to work on next?

Effective Agile prioritisation combines value-based decision-making with practical delivery sequencing.

It is the continuous process of deciding what should be worked on next, what should wait and what should not be done at all.

Prioritisation happens at different levels

Prioritisation is not limited to arranging user stories in a backlog.

Product goals. At the highest level, the organisation decides which outcomes matter most. These might include increasing customer retention, reducing operating costs, improving service reliability, entering a new market or meeting a regulatory obligation. Product goals give lower-level decisions their context.

Roadmaps and initiatives. A roadmap identifies significant problems, opportunities and outcomes the product intends to address. Prioritising a roadmap means deciding where investment should be directed. It should not mean committing every underlying feature to a fixed date before enough discovery has taken place.

Epics and capabilities. Large capabilities compete for funding, technical expertise and delivery capacity. At this level, teams usually compare strategic alignment, expected benefits, cost, uncertainty and delivery risk. Prioritising an epic does not mean every item inside it should automatically be delivered before all other work.

Product Backlog items. The Product Backlog contains potential work that may improve the product. Items should be ordered according to their value, urgency, risk, dependencies and delivery context. A backlog should not become a permanent storage area for every request the organisation has ever made.

Sprint-level decisions. Once work enters a Sprint, Developers manage the Sprint Backlog and decide how best to achieve the Sprint Goal. They may sequence work around technical constraints, collaboration, testing or newly discovered information. They should not be expected to take tickets mechanically from the top of a list.

Defects and incidents. A visual defect, a security vulnerability and a production outage should not receive the same treatment. Teams need clear criteria for determining when unplanned work should interrupt planned delivery.

These levels inform one another in both directions — a goal shapes a backlog, and what a team learns delivering a story can reshape the goal. Open any level for the decision it makes, its planning horizon, who contributes and an example.

The order is a reading aid, not a one-way chain of command.

Who contributes to prioritisation?

In Scrum, the Product Owner is accountable for ordering the Product Backlog.

That does not mean the Product Owner should make every decision alone. Effective prioritisation draws on several perspectives:

  • Product Owners and Product Managers connect work to product goals and customer outcomes.
  • Developers explain technical complexity, delivery options and sequencing constraints.
  • Architects identify structural risks and enabling work.
  • Customers and users provide evidence about actual needs.
  • Business stakeholders contribute commercial, operational and regulatory context.
  • Scrum Masters and Agile Delivery Leads improve the transparency and quality of the decision-making process.

Accountability provides clarity. Collaboration provides better information.

The backlog should be ordered, not merely labelled

A useful Product Backlog has a clear order.

People should be able to understand which items are most likely to be considered next and why.

This does not mean every distant item needs detailed acceptance criteria or an exact score. Near-term items should normally be better understood than distant possibilities.

The backlog should also be kept current. Remove or revisit items that are:

  • Duplicated
  • Outdated
  • No longer strategically relevant
  • Based on disproved assumptions
  • Superseded by another solution
  • Unlikely to be delivered
  • Too vague to support a meaningful decision

Refinement helps teams expose value, scope, assumptions, dependencies and delivery options. This is explored further in effective refinement that prepares work without over-documenting it.

Priority and rank are not the same

The distinction between priority and rank is simple but important.

Priority

The relative importance, value or urgency of an item. A backlog can contain several High-priority items.

Rank

Its exact position in the ordered backlog or delivery sequence. It cannot contain several items in first place.

Priority labels describe the strength of a need. Rank forces a decision about the sequence.

Example: introducing online card payments

A digital product team wants customers to pay outstanding balances online.

The ability to make card payments provides the greatest customer and business value. However, the payment provider requires authentication to be implemented before payments can be processed.

Rank

Backlog item · priority · reason

1. Implement payment-provider authentication High Required before payment processing can be completed.

2. Allow customers to make card payments Critical Highest customer and business value, but depends on authentication.

3. Add payment-failure monitoring and alerts High Reduces operational and support risk.

4. Improve the payment-confirmation page Medium Valuable usability improvement but not required to release.

5. Add downloadable payment receipts Medium Useful but offers lower immediate value.

“Allow customers to make card payments” has the greatest priority, but it is ranked second.

The authentication work has not suddenly become more valuable. It is ranked first because it unlocks the more valuable outcome.

Rank converts value and constraints into an executable sequence.

Dependencies should be challenged

Dependencies should influence prioritisation, but they should not be accepted without investigation. Ask whether a dependency can be:

  • Removed
  • Reduced
  • Mocked
  • Replaced with a temporary solution
  • Worked on in parallel
  • Split into a smaller dependency

For example, the team may be able to build the payment journey using mocked provider responses while production authentication is being completed.

The goal is not to pretend that dependencies do not exist. It is to prevent assumptions and established habits from controlling the delivery plan.

A practical principle

Priority tells the team why an item matters. Rank tells the team where it sits in the current sequence. Strong prioritisation needs both.

The most important outcome is not always the first item that can be delivered, but the delivery sequence should always help the team reach that outcome.
