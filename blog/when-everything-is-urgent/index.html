Managing conflicting stakeholder priorities in Agile delivery

Prioritisation becomes difficult when different stakeholders use different definitions of value.

Sales may focus on a prospect commitment.

Customer Support may focus on reducing customer contacts.

Compliance may focus on a regulatory deadline.

Engineering may focus on an unsupported platform component.

Each concern may be legitimate.

The problem begins when requests are managed through private lists, informal promises and direct interruptions to the delivery team.

Why every request becomes urgent

Stakeholders often describe work as urgent because they believe lower-priority work will never be delivered.

They may also lack visibility of:

  • Existing commitments
  • Team capacity
  • Technical dependencies
  • Product goals
  • Competing risks

The consequence of adding more work

Calling an item urgent can become the only available way to attract attention.

The solution is not to dismiss the stakeholder.

It is to create a transparent decision process.

Different definitions of value

Departments naturally see value through different lenses.

Sales may value:

  • Winning a new customer
  • Protecting a renewal
  • Responding to a market opportunity
  • Fulfilling a commercial promise

Customer Support

Support may value:

  • Reduced contact volume
  • Faster issue resolution
  • Fewer recurring complaints
  • Reduced manual work

Compliance may value:

  • Meeting a regulatory date
  • Producing appropriate evidence
  • Reducing legal exposure
  • Maintaining permission to operate

Engineering may value:

  • Reducing security risk
  • Improving reliability
  • Replacing unsupported technology
  • Protecting future delivery capability

A productive conversation does not ask which department matters most.

It asks how the requested work supports product outcomes and what happens if it is delayed.

Agree common criteria

Significant requests should be compared using shared criteria.

These might include:

  • Product-goal alignment
  • Customer evidence
  • Commercial impact
  • Urgency
  • Consequence of delay
  • Risk reduction
  • Regulatory or contractual need
  • Effort
  • Confidence
  • Dependencies
  • Opportunity cost

The criteria should support a conversation, not become a bureaucratic form that every small request must complete.

Ask what happens if the work is delayed

"Urgent" is difficult to compare.

Consequences are more useful.

Ask:

  • Will revenue be lost?
  • Will a customer be unable to operate?
  • Will a regulatory obligation be missed?
  • Will manual work continue?
  • Will a market opportunity expire?
  • Will a security risk remain exposed?
  • Will the effect be inconvenient or material?

This separates urgency from preference.

It may also reveal that only part of the request is time critical.

A Compliance stakeholder may request a complete reporting suite by a fixed date. Further discussion may show that only the consent record and basic evidence export are required immediately.

Separate evidence from opinion

Prioritisation improves when people distinguish between:

  • What is known
  • What has been observed
  • What has been estimated
  • What has been promised
  • What is assumed
  • What remains uncertain

A Sales request supported by a signed contractual commitment is different from one based on the possibility that a prospect may be interested.

A Support request supported by contact volume and handling-time data is easier to assess than "customers keep complaining".

Evidence does not remove judgement, but it improves the quality of the judgement.

A reasoned example order

Use one visible route for requests

Requests commonly arrive through:

  • Emails
  • Meetings
  • Chat messages
  • Direct conversations with Developers
  • Executive escalations
  • Separate departmental backlogs

This produces hidden priorities and conflicting commitments.

New requests should enter through one understood route.

This could be:

  • A Product Backlog intake
  • A regular prioritisation session
  • A lightweight request board
  • A product discovery process
  • An incident process for urgent operational work

The route should not delay genuine emergencies.

Its purpose is to make requests visible and ensure they are compared with existing work.

Make trade-offs visible

When one item moves up, something else normally moves down.

Stakeholders should be able to see:

  • What changed
  • Why it changed
  • Which evidence influenced the decision
  • Which work has been delayed
  • What consequence the delay may have
  • When the order will next be reviewed

A decision to prioritise a Sales request may be reasonable.

The problem arises when the request is added without showing that a reliability improvement or customer problem has been delayed.

Seniority should not hide the trade-off

Executive requests deserve prompt attention.

They should not bypass the need to understand impact.

A useful response is:

We can move this item to the top. Doing so will delay the consent release by approximately one Sprint and retain the identified compliance risk for longer. Is that the intended trade-off?

This is not resistance.

It is responsible delivery management.

Be explicit about what will not be done

A backlog containing every request creates the impression that all work will eventually be delivered.

Strong prioritisation includes saying:

  • Not now
  • Not within this release
  • Not until further evidence exists
  • Not in the proposed form
  • Not while a dependency remains unresolved
  • Not at all

A clear decision is more respectful than leaving an item indefinitely near the bottom of an unmanageable backlog.

Practical stakeholder questions

Ask:

  1. What outcome are you trying to achieve?
  2. Who benefits?
  3. What evidence supports the request?
  4. What happens if it is delayed?
  5. Is there a genuine deadline?
  6. What is the smallest acceptable outcome?
  7. Which assumptions are being made?
  8. What would change your view?
  9. What current work should move down?
  10. How will we know whether the request delivered value?
Good stakeholder management does not remove disagreement. It makes the disagreement visible, evidence-based and connected to an explicit trade-off.
