Retrospectives

Retrospectives that lead to action

26 Jun 2026 · 22 min read

Turning honest feedback into owned, measurable improvements

The team finishes its retrospective with a board full of comments.

There are frustrations about testing, slow reviews, unclear requirements, meetings, dependencies and changing priorities. Everyone votes. Several actions are written down.

Two weeks later, little has changed.

The next retrospective raises many of the same issues.

This is one of the most common ways retrospectives lose credibility. The team is encouraged to provide honest feedback, but that feedback does not result in visible improvement.

Eventually, people stop contributing.

They write safe, generic comments such as:

  • Communication could be better.
  • Testing should start earlier.
  • We need clearer requirements.
  • Meetings need to be more focused.
  • The team should collaborate more.

None of these statements is necessarily wrong. They are simply too broad to guide a useful adaptation.

A productive retrospective must move beyond collecting opinions.

It should help the team:

  1. Understand what happened.
  2. Separate evidence from assumptions.
  3. Identify the most important issue or opportunity.
  4. Explore what may be causing it.
  5. Agree a small, realistic change.
  6. Decide how the change will be tested.
  7. Review whether it produced the intended result.
Feedback becomes actionable when the team can identify what it will change, why it expects the change to help and how it will know whether it worked.

The purpose of a Sprint Retrospective

The purpose of the Sprint Retrospective is to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness.

The Scrum Team inspects the previous Sprint with regard to:

  • Individuals
  • Interactions
  • Processes
  • Tools
  • The Definition of Done

The team discusses what went well, what problems it encountered and how those problems were — or were not — resolved.

It then identifies the most helpful changes it can make. The most impactful improvements should be addressed as soon as possible and may be added to the Sprint Backlog for the next Sprint.

This means a retrospective is not merely:

  • A meeting for expressing frustration
  • A team-satisfaction survey
  • A Scrum Master's status review
  • A report for management
  • A board containing Start, Stop and Continue columns
  • A compulsory ceremony that ends when the timer expires

The discussion matters, but the purpose is adaptation.

A retrospective should alter how the team approaches future work.

Feedback is not the same as an action

Teams often move directly from a broad complaint to a broad solution.

For example:

Testing happens too late.

The team responds with:

Test earlier.

The action repeats the original concern without explaining what will change.

A more useful sequence separates four things.

Observation

What actually happened?

During the last three Sprints, seven items first entered functional testing during the final two days of the Sprint.

Impact

What consequence did that create?

Four items were not completed, defects were discovered too late to address safely and testing became concentrated under end-of-Sprint pressure.

Insight or hypothesis

What does the team currently believe is contributing to the problem?

The team is starting too many items and treating testing as an activity that begins after development rather than as part of delivery.

Experiment

What will the team change?

During the next Sprint, the team will limit Product Backlog items in active development to three and involve a tester when the first acceptance test is prepared. The team will review whether items enter testing earlier and whether fewer items remain unfinished.

This action is not guaranteed to solve the problem.

It is still better because it is:

  • Specific
  • Small enough to try
  • Connected to an observed issue
  • Based on an explicit hypothesis
  • Measurable
  • Time-bound
  • Reviewable

A retrospective does not need to produce certainty.

It needs to produce a responsible next step.

Psychological safety comes before useful feedback

A retrospective cannot produce honest evidence when people believe speaking openly will create punishment, embarrassment or damage to their career.

Psychological safety means people believe they can raise concerns, acknowledge mistakes, ask questions and challenge assumptions without interpersonal punishment.

It does not mean:

  • Every idea must be accepted.
  • Nobody can be challenged.
  • Difficult behaviour is ignored.
  • The team avoids accountability.
  • Every conversation must remain comfortable.

A psychologically safe team can disagree strongly while still treating people with respect.

Research into team effectiveness has consistently highlighted psychological safety, dependability and clarity as important team conditions. Relevant safety questions include whether people can raise difficult issues, ask for help and make mistakes without those mistakes being held against them.

Signs that a retrospective may not be safe

Watch for patterns such as:

  • The same two people dominate every discussion.
  • Junior team members rarely disagree with senior colleagues.
  • Comments remain generic despite visible problems.
  • Feedback appears only through anonymous submissions.
  • Nobody mentions an issue until a particular person leaves the room.
  • The team discusses external problems but never its own behaviour.
  • People make jokes instead of raising concerns directly.
  • Previous feedback has been shared outside the team without agreement.
  • Actions are used to identify who should be blamed.
  • Managers attend and defend every organisational decision.

A different retrospective format will not fix a lack of safety by itself.

The facilitator may need to:

  • Use silent contribution before open discussion.
  • Gather information privately before the session.
  • Clarify how retrospective information will be handled.
  • Address dominant behaviour.
  • Invite quieter participants deliberately.
  • Run smaller discussions before a whole-team conversation.
  • Deal with a sensitive interpersonal matter outside the group.
  • Escalate an organisational safety issue through an appropriate route.
  • Ask whether the current attendees are preventing honest discussion.

Anonymous input can help expose a concern.

It should not become the team's permanent substitute for being able to speak with one another.

Can the team raise the difficult issue?

Educational pulse — nothing is stored

1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree. This should begin a voluntary conversation, never evaluate an individual.

Establish how retrospective information will be used

People need confidence that their comments will not later appear in a performance review, management report or conversation stripped of context.

The team should agree:

  • Which information remains within the Scrum Team
  • Which actions can be shared
  • Whether themes can be reported without identifying contributors
  • How organisational impediments will be escalated
  • Who will communicate an agreed request
  • What evidence can be shown externally
  • When wider participants may be invited

A useful distinction is:

The team may share the improvement it needs without sharing every personal comment that led to it.

For example, the team might communicate:

The team needs access to a stable integration environment during the first week of each Sprint. Twelve working days were lost during the previous month because the shared environment was unavailable.

It does not need to publish a transcript of who criticised the environment team or what every participant said.

Confidentiality should not be used to hide serious misconduct, safeguarding concerns or legal issues. Those require the appropriate organisational route.

Use evidence to improve the conversation

Retrospectives should consider experience and emotion, but they should not rely entirely on memory.

People naturally remember recent, frustrating or unusual events more easily than routine patterns.

Useful retrospective evidence may include:

  • Sprint Goal outcomes
  • Work completed and unfinished
  • Work item age
  • Cycle time
  • Throughput
  • Work in progress
  • Blocked time
  • Defects
  • Production incidents
  • Reopened work
  • Review waiting time
  • Deployment failures
  • Support demand
  • Unexpected interruptions
  • Dependency delays
  • Changes to Sprint scope
  • Team availability
  • Previous action outcomes

Metrics should support investigation, not deliver the verdict.

A chart showing increased cycle time cannot explain the cause by itself. It can help the team ask better questions:

  • Did items become larger?
  • Was testing delayed?
  • Did review queues grow?
  • Were dependencies unavailable?
  • Did urgent work interrupt the Sprint?
  • Did work remain open while waiting for a decision?

The team should combine quantitative evidence with qualitative experience.

A Developer may explain that a review waited for three days because only one person understood the affected component. That context matters as much as the number.

Separate observation from interpretation

Consider this comment:

The Product Owner keeps changing their mind.

This is an interpretation and may contain an assumption about intent.

A more useful observation might be:

Three Product Backlog items had their acceptance criteria changed after development began during the last Sprint.

The team can now explore:

  • What information triggered the changes?
  • Were the items sufficiently refined?
  • Did customer feedback arrive?
  • Was the original outcome unclear?
  • Were changes necessary to protect the Sprint Goal?
  • Did the team begin work despite known uncertainty?
  • Was the Product Owner responding to stakeholder pressure?
  • Could the work have been split into a smaller experiment?

The original comment attributes the problem to a person.

The observation creates room to understand the system.

Giving feedback about behaviour

Not every retrospective issue is a process problem.

Individual behaviour can affect:

  • Collaboration
  • Decision-making
  • Inclusion
  • Review quality
  • Psychological safety
  • Knowledge sharing
  • Team reliability

Feedback about behaviour should remain specific and avoid assumptions about personality or motivation.

A useful structure is Situation–Behaviour–Impact:

  1. Describe the specific situation.
  2. Describe the observable behaviour.
  3. Explain the impact.

For example:

During Tuesday's refinement session, three people were interrupted before completing their explanations. This meant their technical concerns were not explored, and two of them stopped contributing to the discussion.

This is more useful than:

You are dismissive and do not listen.

The conversation can then include an inquiry about intent rather than assuming why the behaviour occurred. The Situation–Behaviour–Impact approach is designed to keep feedback focused on observable behaviour and its effect while reducing judgement.

When personal feedback belongs outside the retrospective

A retrospective should not become a public performance review.

Handle feedback privately when:

  • It concerns one person and does not require a team discussion.
  • Public discussion would create unnecessary embarrassment.
  • The issue involves confidential employment information.
  • A manager needs to address an individual responsibility.
  • A serious conduct concern requires a formal process.
  • The affected person has not had a fair opportunity to understand the feedback.

The team can still examine the wider system.

For example, an individual repeatedly misses code reviews. A private conversation may be appropriate, while the retrospective considers whether review ownership, availability and expectations are clear.

Generate insights before choosing actions

Teams often vote on comments and immediately begin proposing solutions.

This can create actions that address symptoms rather than causes.

Suppose the most popular comment is:

Code reviews take too long.

Possible causes include:

  • Too many items are open simultaneously.
  • Review requests are too large.
  • Ownership is concentrated in one specialist.
  • Review expectations are unclear.
  • Review work is treated as lower priority than development.
  • Notifications are missed.
  • Reviewers lack product context.
  • Time-zone differences create delays.
  • The team is waiting for approval rather than peer review.
  • Automated checks are insufficient.

Each cause suggests a different response.

"Review pull requests faster" does not address any of them.

Useful insight techniques include:

  • Asking why the pattern occurs
  • Mapping the delivery sequence
  • Identifying where work waits
  • Comparing Sprints
  • Examining a specific example
  • Separating controllable factors from external constraints
  • Looking for assumptions
  • Comparing expected and actual behaviour
  • Exploring what is different when the problem does not occur

The purpose is not to conduct an endless root-cause investigation.

The team needs enough understanding to choose a sensible experiment.

Prioritise the improvement

A retrospective may identify ten genuine problems.

The team is unlikely to improve all ten during the next Sprint.

Selecting too many actions creates another backlog of unfinished work.

Prioritise improvement opportunities by considering:

  • Expected impact
  • Frequency of the problem
  • Consequence of doing nothing
  • Ability to influence the issue
  • Effort required
  • Learning value
  • Connection to the Sprint or Product Goal
  • Effect on quality
  • Effect on team safety
  • Whether the issue blocks other improvements

Teams should usually choose one or two meaningful improvements rather than producing a long list of low-commitment actions.

Improvement work needs a work-in-progress limit too.

Work within control, influence and concern

Not every problem can be solved entirely by the Scrum Team.

Classify issues into three broad areas.

Control

The team can change the situation directly. Examples: work-in-progress limits, review practices, refinement structure, test collaboration, meeting facilitation, pairing, working agreements.

Influence

The team cannot make the final decision but can affect it. Examples: requesting access to an environment, negotiating support from a specialist, showing the impact of a governance delay, proposing a change to an organisational process, agreeing an interface with another team.

Concern

The team has little realistic ability to alter the situation at present. Examples might include a wider company restructure, a fixed legal constraint, a supplier decision outside the contract, a budget decision already made.

The team should avoid spending the entire retrospective repeatedly discussing an area it cannot currently change.

That does not mean ignoring organisational impediments.

An actionable influence item might be:

The Scrum Master and one Developer will present the last three months of environment downtime to the Platform Lead by Wednesday and request a named support rota. The team will review the response at the next retrospective.

"Management should fix the environments" is not an action.

Turn actions into experiments

Complex delivery problems rarely have guaranteed solutions.

Treating improvements as experiments encourages learning rather than pretending the team already knows the answer.

A useful retrospective experiment contains:

  • Problem: What pattern are we addressing?
  • Evidence: What have we observed?
  • Hypothesis: Why do we believe this change may help?
  • Experiment: What will we do differently?
  • Owner: Who will ensure the experiment progresses?
  • Measure: What signal will we inspect?
  • Duration: How long will we try it?
  • Review point: When will we decide what happened?
  • Decision: Will we adopt, adapt or stop the change?

Example

Problem: Code reviews are delaying completion.

Evidence: During the last two Sprints, the median time waiting for first review was 19 hours. Four items waited longer than two working days.

Hypothesis: Large review requests and unclear review ownership are increasing waiting time.

Experiment: For the next Sprint:

  • Changes expected to exceed 400 lines will be discussed before implementation.
  • Each active item will have a named first reviewer.
  • Review requests will be raised before 3 pm where practical.

Owner: One Developer will maintain visibility of review age, but the team collectively owns the behaviour.

Measure:

  • Median time to first review
  • Number of requests waiting more than one working day
  • Team feedback on review quality

Review point: Next Sprint Retrospective.

Decision: Adopt, adapt or stop the approach based on the evidence.

This is stronger than:

Developers should review code more quickly.

Ownership does not mean assigning blame

Every action needs enough ownership to avoid becoming everybody's responsibility and therefore nobody's work.

An owner should:

  • Keep the action visible
  • Coordinate any required conversation
  • Raise blockers
  • Gather agreed evidence
  • Ensure the outcome is reviewed

The owner does not necessarily perform every part of the improvement.

A team-wide experiment can still have one person responsible for follow-through.

The Scrum Master should not automatically own every retrospective action. Doing so teaches the team that continuous improvement belongs to the facilitator rather than the Scrum Team.

Ownership should match the action.

Examples include:

  • A Developer coordinates a review experiment.
  • The Product Owner clarifies a stakeholder-intake process.
  • The Scrum Master escalates an organisational impediment.
  • The whole team applies a work-in-progress limit.
  • Two team members investigate an automation option.

Make improvement work visible

Retrospective actions often fail because they sit in meeting notes that nobody checks.

Improvement work should be visible in the team's normal system.

Depending on the action, it may be:

  • Added to the Sprint Backlog
  • Added to an improvement board
  • Included in a working agreement
  • Scheduled as a team conversation
  • Tracked as an organisational impediment
  • Included in the Definition of Done
  • Added to an automation or engineering backlog

The method should match the nature of the action.

A five-minute behaviour change does not need an elaborate ticket.

A technical improvement requiring several days of effort does need to be considered alongside other Sprint work.

Retrospective actions do not receive unlimited hidden capacity. If the team agrees that an improvement matters, it must create space for it.

This connects directly to using the Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog to make deliberate delivery decisions.

Review previous actions first

A retrospective should not begin as though the previous one never happened.

Review:

  • What did we agree to try?
  • Was the experiment performed?
  • What evidence did we collect?
  • What changed?
  • What did not change?
  • What did we learn?
  • Should we adopt, adapt or stop the practice?

An action being completed does not prove that it worked.

For example:

We created a review rota.

This only proves that a rota was created.

The intended outcome may have been:

Reduce the time work waits for review.

The team should examine whether waiting time actually decreased.

If an action was not completed, ask why:

  • Was it too large?
  • Was ownership unclear?
  • Was no capacity created?
  • Did the team stop believing it was valuable?
  • Was it blocked?
  • Did the issue disappear?
  • Was the action itself ineffective?

Repeatedly creating actions without reviewing them teaches the team that retrospective commitments are optional.

Action completed versus outcome improved

Keep the distinction visible.

Examples contrasting a completed action with whether the intended outcome actually improved.

Choosing the right retrospective format

A format is a facilitation tool, not the purpose of the retrospective.

Start, Stop, Continue

Useful when the team needs a simple, broad review of current practices.

Risk: It can produce generic lists without sufficient evidence or analysis.

The Four Ls

The team considers what it liked, learned, lacked and longed for.

Useful when learning and team experience need more attention.

Risk: It can remain reflective without producing a focused action.

Timeline

The team reconstructs significant events across the Sprint. Useful when memories differ, several disruptions occurred, the sequence of events matters, or the team needs a shared factual baseline.

Sailboat or journey metaphor

The team explores the destination, what moved it forward, what slowed it down and risks ahead. Useful for connecting improvement to a goal.

Risk: The metaphor can consume more attention than the actual issue.

Cause analysis

The team examines one significant problem in depth. Useful when the issue is recurring, its impact is high, previous actions have failed, or the team needs to understand contributing conditions.

Data-led retrospective

The discussion begins with delivery, quality or operational evidence. Useful when conversations are dominated by perception, a recurring pattern needs examination, or flow or quality is deteriorating.

Risk: Metrics can silence important human experience if presented as the only truth.

Future-focused retrospective

The team imagines a more effective next Sprint and works backwards to identify changes. Useful when the team is tired of repeatedly discussing the same past problems.

No format guarantees a useful result.

Choose the format based on the question the team needs to answer.

Facilitation practices that improve feedback

Create participation before discussion

Give everyone time to think and contribute before open conversation begins. Silent writing can prevent the first speaker from shaping every response.

Ask for examples

When somebody says "Communication was poor," ask:

  • When did this occur?
  • What information was missing?
  • Who needed it?
  • What happened as a result?
  • Was there an example where communication worked well?

Balance positive and corrective feedback

Useful retrospectives identify practices worth preserving as well as problems to solve. The team should understand why something worked so that it can repeat the conditions.

Do not defend immediately

When feedback is raised, resist the urge to explain why the situation was unavoidable. Clarify the observation and impact first. An explanation may later become relevant, but immediate defence can close the conversation.

Manage dominant voices

Use silent contribution, round-robin input, small-group discussion, time-limited contributions and direct invitation to quieter participants.

Do not force people to speak about sensitive issues before they are ready.

Rotate facilitation

The Scrum Master can facilitate the retrospective, but they do not need to facilitate every exercise forever. Rotating facilitation can build team capability, introduce new perspectives, reduce dependency on one person and allow the Scrum Master to participate more fully.

The facilitator still needs sufficient skill to manage safety, conflict and inclusion.

Close with confidence and clarity

Before ending, ask:

  • What exactly have we agreed?
  • Who will keep it visible?
  • When will it be reviewed?
  • How confident are we that we will perform the experiment?
  • What could prevent it?
  • Does anything need to enter the Sprint Backlog?
  • What may be shared outside the team?

Remote and hybrid retrospectives

Remote and hybrid teams face additional risks.

People in the room may dominate those joining remotely. Side conversations may exclude others. Digital boards can create a false sense of equal participation while some people struggle to follow several conversations.

Use practices such as:

  • One shared digital workspace for everyone
  • Individual writing before discussion
  • Clear turn-taking
  • Deliberate pauses
  • Written summaries of emerging decisions
  • Equal access to voting
  • Cameras as a choice rather than proof of engagement
  • Time-zone-aware scheduling
  • Breaks during longer sessions
  • Explicit checks for remote participants before moving on

Avoid asking remote participants to watch a physical whiteboard through a distant camera.

The facilitation method should make participation equally practical, not merely technically possible.

Common retrospective anti-patterns

The same format is used every Sprint

Why it happens: The format is familiar and easy to prepare.

Why it causes difficulty: The team begins completing the exercise mechanically rather than examining the most important issue.

Corrective action: Choose a format based on the purpose of the session.

The retrospective becomes a complaint session

Why it happens: People have no other safe route for raising concerns.

Why it causes difficulty: Frustration is repeated without creating adaptation.

Corrective action: Acknowledge the impact, identify what can be controlled or influenced and convert the highest-value concern into an experiment.

Actions are vague

Why it happens: The team runs out of time or avoids making a specific commitment.

Why it causes difficulty: Nobody can tell what behaviour should change.

Corrective action: Define the experiment, owner, measure and review point.

Too many actions are created

Why it happens: The team wants to respond to every contribution.

Why it causes difficulty: Improvement capacity is spread too thinly and most actions remain unfinished.

Corrective action: Prioritise one or two impactful improvements.

The Scrum Master owns every action

Why it happens: The Scrum Master facilitates the session and becomes the default administrator.

Why it causes difficulty: The team outsources responsibility for its own effectiveness.

Corrective action: Assign ownership according to the work while maintaining collective responsibility.

Previous actions are not reviewed

Why it happens: Each retrospective begins with a fresh board.

Why it causes difficulty: The team cannot learn whether its adaptations worked.

Corrective action: Review previous experiments before gathering new actions.

Actions measure activity rather than effect

Why it happens: Activities are easier to verify.

Why it causes difficulty: The team can complete the action without improving the original problem.

Corrective action: Define the intended outcome and a signal that would indicate change.

The retrospective is used to evaluate individuals

Why it happens: Team feedback and performance management are mixed together.

Why it causes difficulty: People stop being honest and begin protecting themselves.

Corrective action: Discuss observable team impacts and handle individual employment matters through an appropriate private process.

Management attends every retrospective

Why it happens: Leaders want visibility or believe their presence will speed up decisions.

Why it causes difficulty: Power differences may limit candour and turn the event into reporting.

Corrective action: Keep the normal retrospective focused on the Scrum Team. Invite others deliberately for a specific purpose and with the team's agreement.

Nothing can be changed

Why it happens: Organisational impediments dominate the discussion.

Why it causes difficulty: The team becomes resigned and stops identifying smaller areas of influence.

Corrective action: Separate control, influence and concern. Define a specific escalation experiment while still improving what the team controls.

The team skips the retrospective after a successful Sprint

Why it happens: People assume improvement is only required after failure.

Why it causes difficulty: The team misses an opportunity to understand and repeat successful conditions.

Corrective action: Examine what enabled the success and what new risk or opportunity should be addressed.

A practical retrospective structure

The following structure can be adapted to the team and Sprint length.

  1. Review previous experiments. Inspect what the team agreed last time and whether it produced the intended result.
  2. Establish the conditions for discussion. Check whether people feel able to contribute and clarify how information will be handled.
  3. Gather evidence. Collect events, experiences, outcomes and relevant delivery or quality data.
  4. Generate insights. Explore patterns, impacts, assumptions and possible causes.
  5. Select the most helpful improvement. Prioritise the opportunity with the strongest combination of impact, urgency, influence and learning value.
  6. Design the experiment. Define the problem, evidence, hypothesis, change, owner, measure, duration and review point.
  7. Plan the work. Decide whether the experiment needs capacity, a Sprint Backlog item, a working-agreement change or an external conversation.
  8. Close the feedback loop. Confirm what will be reviewed at the next retrospective and what may be communicated outside the team.

An actionable improvement template

Use the following structure:

  • Problem or opportunity: What pattern should improve?
  • Evidence: What did the team observe?
  • Impact: Why does it matter?
  • Hypothesis: Why does the team believe the proposed change may help?
  • Experiment: What will the team do differently?
  • Owner: Who will keep the experiment visible and coordinate follow-through?
  • Measure: What evidence will indicate whether the outcome changed?
  • Duration: How long will the team try it?
  • Review date: When will the result be inspected?
  • Decision: Will the team adopt, adapt or stop the practice?

Measuring whether retrospectives are working

Do not judge retrospective quality by:

  • Number of sticky notes
  • Number of actions created
  • How entertaining the format was
  • Whether every person spoke for the same length of time
  • Whether velocity increased
  • Whether the meeting ended early

Useful signals include:

  • Percentage of experiments actually reviewed
  • Number of unfinished improvement actions
  • Recurrence of previously discussed problems
  • Time taken to resolve impediments
  • Reduction in blocked time
  • Reduction in avoidable rework
  • Improved flow
  • Improved Definition of Done adherence
  • Fewer repeated production incidents
  • Greater confidence in raising difficult issues
  • More balanced participation
  • Evidence that successful practices are being preserved
  • Team confidence that retrospectives lead to change

Be careful with measurement.

A safety pulse should not become a performance target. People should not feel pressured to report that they feel safe.

Measures should create questions, not punishments.

Conclusion

A retrospective does not create improvement merely because it took place.

Improvement happens when the team:

  • Surfaces honest evidence
  • Understands the impact
  • Explores causes rather than blaming people
  • Selects a small number of meaningful changes
  • Creates space to perform them
  • Measures whether they helped
  • Reviews the result
  • Adapts again

The feedback does not need to be perfectly worded.

The action does not need to guarantee success.

It does need to be specific enough to try and important enough to review.

The value of a retrospective is not the feedback collected in the room. It is the change the team is willing to test after leaving it.
