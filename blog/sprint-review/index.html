Ceremonies

Running a Sprint Review

23 Jul 2026 · 5 min read

The Sprint Review is the one hour a sprint where working software meets the people it is for — and the demo is its heart. Done well it earns trust and sharpens the plan; done badly it is a status meeting with a projector. Here is how I run one, and how I read the room it is in.

A Sprint Review is not a status update

The Sprint Review — with the demo at its heart — exists to put working software in front of the people it affects and get their honest reaction while there is still time to act on it. It is an inspect-and-adapt moment, not a performance and not a progress report. The whole value sits in that feedback loop: show real work, provoke a real reaction, and let it change the plan.

Waste it as theatre

A rehearsed, polished walk-through that invites applause instead of feedback. Nothing is learned, so nothing changes.

Waste it as a status meeting

Slides, percentages and a burndown, but no software. The room cannot react to a number the way it reacts to something it can use.

A Sprint Review is more than the demo — it also covers progress toward the Product Goal and adapting the backlog. But the demo is the part that makes all of that concrete, so it is where this guide spends most of its time.

Know who is in the room

The single biggest lever on a Sprint Review is who is watching — and most get it wrong by showing everyone the same thing. Before you prepare anything, list who will be there and what each of them actually needs to see.

Product Owner

Wants Work that meets the acceptance criteria and moves the Sprint Goal.

Show them Finished work held up against the goal you set.

Lose them by Surprises — work they are seeing for the very first time.

Business stakeholders & sponsors

Wants Value, outcomes, cost and timing.

Show them What the work enables, in their language, tied to money or risk.

Lose them by Internal jargon and screens they cannot connect to an outcome.

End users & customers

Wants Does this solve my problem, and can I actually use it.

Show them A real task, start to finish, on realistic data.

Lose them by Idealised happy-paths and "imagine this said your name".

The delivery team

Wants To see the pieces integrate, and shared pride in the increment.

Show them How the parts fit together into one working whole.

Lose them by One person narrating while everyone else sits silent.

Leadership & executives

Wants Progress against strategy, plus confidence and risk.

Show them Honest progress toward the goal, including what is not done and why.

Lose them by Vanity metrics and over-promising you pay for next sprint.

Dependent teams

Wants Interfaces, contracts and timing.

Show them The integration points and what is ready to build on.

Lose them by Assuming they carry your context.

Most rooms are a mix. You cannot give six demos at once, so lead with the outcome for the many and keep the technical depth one question away for the few who want it. Read the room as you go: pitch to the people who decide what happens next, and let the specialists pull you deeper when they need to.

Prepare the path, not a script

Preparation is the difference between a demo that lands and one that limps — but the goal is a known-good path through the software, not a rehearsed performance.

Decide the two or three things worth showing, and cut the rest.

Tie each one to the Sprint Goal — if it does not serve the goal, it does not need stage time.

Use realistic data. "asdf" and "Test Test" quietly tell the room this is not real.

Have the environment ready and open before people arrive — not the moment they do.

Decide who shows what, so it flows between voices instead of stalling.

Have a fallback: a short recording or screenshots for anything fragile.

A simple shape for the hour

Keep it to about thirty to forty-five minutes with real time for discussion. A shape I trust:

01 Set the scene. Restate the Sprint Goal and what you set out to do.

02 Show the increment. Real workflows, real data, outcome first.

03 Invite feedback. Ask specific questions, not "any thoughts?".

04 Capture it. Write feedback where it will be acted on, in front of the room.

05 Close. Confirm what is Done, what changes, and what is next.

The dos

Show working software, not slides. The increment is the point; if there is nothing to run, that is the story.

Tie everything to the Sprint Goal and to value. Every item should answer "so what?".

Use realistic data and real scenarios. Credibility lives in the details.

Let the people who built it show it. Ownership shows, and questions get real answers.

Make feedback safe and specific. Ask targeted questions, and thank people for the hard ones.

Be honest about what is not done. Inspecting the truth is the entire exercise.

Timebox and keep momentum. Protect the hour; park the deep dives.

Capture feedback into the backlog. If it is not written down, it did not happen.

The don'ts

Don't turn it into a status meeting. Percentages and Gantt charts are not a demo.

Don't demo work that isn't Done. Half-built features invite feedback on the wrong things.

Don't hide what broke or slipped. A concealed problem surfaces later, and worse.

Don't read from a script or over-rehearse. A suspiciously perfect demo reads as a staged one.

Don't spring surprises on the Product Owner. The review confirms shared work; it does not reveal it.

Don't let one comment hijack the room. Note it, park it, move on.

Don't get defensive about feedback. You asked for it; the reaction is the product.

Don't let feedback evaporate. Unowned, unrecorded feedback trains people to stop giving it.

When something breaks

It will, eventually. A live failure handled calmly builds more trust than a flawless run — it proves the demo is real, not a recording. Reach for your fallback, name the problem plainly, note it for later, and move on. Do not spend the room's time debugging on stage; that is what the rest of the sprint is for.

It earns its keep after it ends

The demo is not finished when the screen goes dark. The Sprint Review turns what you heard into changes to the backlog and the plan — that is the "adapt" half of inspect-and-adapt. If nothing in the next sprint reflects the feedback you gathered, the room will notice, and next time the people whose opinion you most need will quietly stop coming. Feeding that feedback back into a well-ordered backlog is the same discipline as Effective refinement — the demo just gives the backlog its most honest source of signal.
