The factors that shape Agile prioritisation decisions.

A team rarely chooses between one obviously valuable item and several obviously worthless ones.

The difficult decisions involve competing benefits:

  • A feature that could increase revenue
  • A defect affecting existing customers
  • A technical upgrade reducing security risk
  • A contractual commitment with a fixed date
  • A discovery activity that could prevent months of wasted development

There is no universal formula that will always produce the correct order.

Good prioritisation considers several factors and makes the trade-offs visible.

Business and customer value

Business value describes the benefit expected by the organisation. Customer value describes the benefit experienced by the people using or receiving the service.

Relevant forms of value include:

Revenue

Adoption

Retention

Cost reduction

Service improvement

Strategic alignment

Market differentiation

Reduced manual work

Improved customer outcomes

A stakeholder request should not be accepted at face value without understanding the outcome behind it.

Proposed solution

“Build a customer dashboard.”

Outcome behind it

“Reduce the number of customers contacting Support for order-status information.”

Once the outcome is understood, the team can consider alternatives. A tracking link or improved notification may solve most of the problem without building a complete dashboard.

Useful questions include:

  • Who benefits?
  • What behaviour should change?
  • What evidence supports the expected value?
  • How will the benefit be measured?
  • Is the requested solution the smallest useful option?

Urgency and time criticality

Urgency describes the consequence of delay.

It is related to value, but the two are not interchangeable.

A valuable capability may remain valuable in six months. A smaller compliance change may have to be completed within six weeks because missing the date has an unacceptable consequence.

Time-sensitive work can include:

Regulatory deadlines

Contractual obligations

Seasonal demand

Market opportunities

Supplier agreement changes

End-of-support dates

Customer commitments

Production issues

Teams should test whether a deadline is genuine.

Ask:

  • What happens if the date is missed?
  • Who established it?
  • Is it fixed or preferred?
  • Is the full proposed scope required by that date?
  • Could a smaller outcome satisfy the immediate need?

Urgency without a meaningful consequence is often a preference presented as a deadline.

Risk reduction

Some work creates value by reducing exposure rather than adding a visible feature.

Relevant risks include:

Operational

Technical

Security

Commercial

Delivery

Compliance

Reputational

A monitoring improvement may not generate revenue, but it could identify failed payments before hundreds of customers are affected.

A technical spike may produce no releasable functionality, but it could prove that a proposed integration is not viable before the organisation invests heavily in it.

Risk should be assessed using both likelihood and impact.

A highly unlikely inconvenience should not automatically displace valuable work. A credible risk with severe consequences may need immediate attention.

Dependencies and sequencing

Dependencies affect when work can be delivered.

Common types include:

Technical

Data

Supplier

Team

Approval

Organisational

A genuine dependency makes delivery impossible or materially unsafe until another condition is satisfied.

A preferred sequence is not necessarily a dependency.

“The backend must be completely finished before the interface can begin” may be an established habit rather than a technical fact.

Teams should ask:

  • What exactly is required?
  • Why is it required?
  • Who owns it?
  • Can an interface be agreed first?
  • Can it be mocked?
  • Can both sides progress in parallel?
  • Can the dependency be reduced?

Dependencies should inform rank, not create passive waiting.

Effort and complexity

Effort matters because capacity is limited.

A relatively small item that creates meaningful value may sensibly rank above a large initiative with similar benefits.

However, effort should not become the sole decision.

Repeatedly choosing the easiest work can leave major customer problems, risks and technical constraints unresolved.

Story points can help teams discuss relative complexity, effort and uncertainty. They do not measure business value and should not be used as a complete prioritisation method.

This distinction is explored in using story points to discuss complexity without treating them as value or time.

The useful question is not simply:

How large is this item?

It is:

Is the expected outcome worth this investment now?

Learning and uncertainty

Sometimes the most valuable next activity is a small piece of learning.

Examples include:

Spikes

Prototypes

Proofs of concept

Customer research

Experiments

Technical discovery

Early validation

Suppose a team is considering a six-month supplier integration.

A short spike may reveal that the supplier’s API cannot support a critical use case. Ranking the spike above the full integration could prevent months of wasted delivery.

Learning work should have a clear question and a decision it is intended to support.

Vague

“Investigate the API.”

Focused

“Determine whether the API supports real-time cancellation updates and recommend whether integration should proceed.”

Technical health

Technical work often loses prioritisation discussions because its value is less visible than a customer-facing feature.

Technical health includes:

Technical debt

Maintenance

Dependency upgrades

Platform improvements

Performance

Observability

Test automation

Deployment automation

Architectural improvements

Technical work should be connected to a consequence. For example:

  • Upgrading an unsupported dependency reduces security risk.
  • Improving observability reduces incident-detection time.
  • Automating deployment reduces release effort and human error.
  • Refactoring a fragile component reduces the risk of upcoming changes.
  • Improving performance protects conversion during peak demand.

This does not mean every technical preference should become a business priority. It means technical work deserves to be assessed through its effect on reliability, cost, risk and future delivery capability.

Defects and incidents

Defects should be prioritised using more than a generic severity label. Consider:

Number of affected users

Frequency

Availability of a workaround

Financial impact

Security impact

Reputational impact

Operational burden

Data integrity

Regulatory consequences

A defect affecting a rarely used screen may be tolerable.

A defect occasionally duplicating financial transactions may need immediate attention, even if only a small number of customers have reported it.

Teams should also consider the cost of workarounds. A customer-facing issue may appear minor while creating hours of manual work for an operations team every day.

Making the trade-off

A prioritisation discussion should not attempt to prove that one factor always matters more than every other factor.

Instead, it should make the decision explicit.

For each significant item, ask:

  • What outcome does it support?
  • What happens if it is delayed?
  • What risk does it reduce?
  • What evidence supports the expected value?
  • What does it depend on?
  • What will it cost to deliver?
  • What uncertainty should be resolved first?
  • What work will wait if this moves up?

The objective is not to remove judgement.

It is to make judgement better informed, more consistent and easier to explain.

The best next item is the one that offers the strongest current combination of outcome, urgency, risk reduction and deliverability—not simply the loudest claim of importance.
