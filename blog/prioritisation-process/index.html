Prioritisation

A practical Agile prioritisation process

17 Jun 2026 · 6 min read

From product goal to a clear, explainable order of work

Prioritisation does not need to involve a complicated scoring model every time two backlog items change position.

The level of analysis should be proportionate to the decision.

Reordering two small, well-understood items may require a short conversation. Committing significant funding to a time-sensitive initiative deserves deeper investigation.

The following process can be adapted for backlog ordering, release planning and stakeholder prioritisation workshops.

1. Confirm the intended outcome

Begin with the product goal or result the team is trying to achieve.

Without this context, prioritisation becomes a comparison of unrelated requests.

Ask:

  • What outcome are we pursuing?
  • Who should benefit?
  • How will we know whether progress has been made?

2. Agree the decision criteria

Select the factors relevant to the decision.

These may include:

  • Customer or business value
  • Strategic alignment
  • Urgency
  • Cost of delay
  • Risk reduction
  • Effort
  • Confidence
  • Dependencies

Use a small set of meaningful criteria rather than an unnecessarily complicated universal model.

3. Gather evidence and identify assumptions

Collect available:

  • Customer research
  • Product data
  • Operational evidence
  • Commercial information
  • Contractual requirements
  • Regulatory information
  • Technical findings
  • Delivery data

Clearly distinguish evidence from estimates and assumptions.

The absence of perfect information should not prevent a decision, but uncertainty should be visible.

4. Assess value, urgency, risk and effort

Compare the likely benefit with:

  • The consequence of delay
  • The risk reduced
  • The delivery investment
  • The confidence in the current understanding

The assessment may be qualitative or numerical.

Numbers are useful when they clarify the discussion. They are harmful when they create an illusion of certainty.

5. Identify dependencies and sequencing constraints

Make relevant dependencies visible.

These may involve:

  • Technology
  • Data
  • Suppliers
  • Other teams
  • Approvals
  • Organisational changes

Describe what is actually required rather than applying a general dependency label.

6. Challenge unnecessary dependencies

Ask whether each dependency can be:

  • Removed
  • Reduced
  • Mocked
  • Split
  • Temporarily replaced
  • Progressed in parallel

Do not design the entire delivery sequence around an unverified assumption.

7. Split work where appropriate

Large items combine several outcomes and make trade-offs difficult.

Split work into smaller, useful outcomes that can be:

  • Assessed independently
  • Delivered earlier
  • Validated with customers
  • Reordered as evidence changes

The goal is not to create more tickets. It is to create smaller decisions and earlier feedback.

8. Rank the items

Create a clear order for the items being actively considered.

Avoid ending the discussion with several items occupying the same effective position.

Priority categories may still be useful, but rank determines the sequence.

9. Validate the order

Review the proposed order with the people who hold relevant information.

Check:

  • Technical feasibility
  • Missing evidence
  • Incorrect assumptions
  • Dependency accuracy
  • Operational consequences
  • Strategic alignment

Validation should improve the decision. It should not reopen every personal preference without evidence.

10. Communicate the trade-offs

Explain:

  • Why the order was chosen
  • What moved
  • What will wait
  • Which risks remain
  • What evidence influenced the decision
  • When the order will be reviewed

When an item moves up, make the displaced work visible.

11. Review when information changes

Prioritisation is continuous.

Review the order when:

  • Product goals change
  • New customer evidence appears
  • An incident occurs
  • A dependency changes
  • A deadline becomes clearer
  • Delivery complexity is better understood
  • An assumption is disproved

Do not preserve an outdated order simply because a workshop once produced it.

Questions teams should ask

Use these questions during refinement and prioritisation discussions:

  • What outcome does this item support?
  • Who benefits?
  • What evidence supports the expected value?
  • What happens if we delay it?
  • Is there a genuine deadline?
  • Why does that deadline exist?
  • Does this reduce a meaningful risk?
  • What must happen before it can be delivered?
  • Can the dependency be removed or reduced?
  • Can the work be split?
  • What is the smallest useful outcome?
  • What are we choosing not to do?
  • What assumptions are we making?
  • What information would change the ranking?
  • How will we know whether value was delivered?

These questions are prompts, not a mandatory form for every ticket.

Measuring whether prioritisation is working

Useful signals include:

  • Progress towards product goals
  • Improved customer outcomes
  • Reduced lead time
  • Faster feedback
  • Reduced work in progress
  • Fewer obsolete backlog items
  • Less unexplained priority churn
  • Fewer dependency delays
  • Better stakeholder understanding
  • Improved predictability
  • Reduced operational risk
  • Delivery of valuable outcomes

Teams should also examine:

  • How often work is interrupted
  • Why priorities change
  • How long valuable work waits
  • How much refined work is abandoned
  • Whether recurring incidents are being addressed
  • Whether technical health is improving
  • Whether delivered changes produce the expected result

Velocity should not be the primary measure of prioritisation quality.

A team can increase velocity while delivering low-value work.

It can also reduce velocity temporarily while resolving a major risk or validating an uncertain direction.

Prioritisation quality is demonstrated by the value and learning created, not by the number of story points completed.

Final principle

Effective prioritisation is the discipline of making deliberate trade-offs.

It means:

  • Choosing outcomes over activity
  • Ordering work rather than merely applying labels
  • Distinguishing priority from rank
  • Making dependencies visible
  • Challenging constraints
  • Using evidence where possible
  • Keeping the backlog current
  • Revisiting decisions when circumstances change
  • Being honest about what will not be done

The objective is not to create a perfect order that never changes.

The objective is to make the best current decision, understand why it was made and remain willing to change it when better information emerges.

Prioritisation is the discipline of making the next best choice — and being clear about the choices left behind.
