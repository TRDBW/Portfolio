Metrics

Agile Metrics That Improve Decisions

Using velocity, flow and quality measures without turning teams into scoreboards

27 Jul 2026 · 10 min read

Teams often begin measuring delivery because they want better answers.

They want to know:

  • When might this work be completed?
  • Where is delivery slowing down?
  • Are we starting too much?
  • Is quality improving?
  • Are customers receiving value sooner?
  • Did the last process change help?

The difficulty begins when a measure stops supporting a decision and becomes the objective itself.

Velocity becomes a target.

Bug counts become a judgement of testers or Developers.

Lead time is reported without defining where the clock starts.

Dashboards grow while nobody changes how work is delivered.

A useful metric should help somebody inspect the system and decide what to do next.

Do not begin with “What can we measure?” Begin with “What decision are we trying to improve?”

Use a balanced set of measures

No single metric can describe whether a product or delivery system is healthy.

A team can have:

  • High velocity but poor customer outcomes
  • Fast cycle time but frequent production failures
  • High throughput because work items became smaller
  • Few reported bugs because customers stopped reporting them
  • Predictable delivery of work that has little value

A useful measurement approach normally considers four areas.

Outcomes

Is the product creating the intended customer or business result?

Flow

How smoothly and predictably does work move from request to completion?

Quality

How often does work require correction, reopening or emergency intervention?

Delivery context

What changed in capacity, scope, item size, workflow or the Definition of Done?

Scrum’s Evidence-Based Management guidance similarly encourages organisations to consider value, time to market and the ability to innovate rather than treating internal output as the complete picture.

Define the measurement boundary

Metric names are not enough.

Two teams may both report “lead time” while measuring entirely different periods.

One may measure:

Request created to released to customers.

Another may measure:

Product Backlog item selected to development complete.

Before interpreting any time-based measure, define:

  • What type of work is included?
  • When does the clock start?
  • When does it stop?
  • Are weekends included?
  • Does waiting time count?
  • What does finished mean?
  • Have workflow policies changed?
  • Are different classes of work mixed together?

The current Kanban Guide defines its flow metrics using explicitly agreed started and finished points within the team’s workflow.

Without those boundaries, comparisons are unreliable.

Velocity

Velocity is commonly calculated as the total story points attached to Product Backlog items completed during a Sprint.

Only work meeting the Definition of Done should contribute.

Velocity can help a stable team consider how much similarly sized work it has recently completed.

It may support:

  • Sprint forecasting
  • Release conversations
  • Identifying unusual variation
  • Discussing whether work is becoming larger or less understood

Velocity should not be used to:

  • Compare teams
  • Assess individual performance
  • Demand continual increases
  • Convert story points into hours
  • Prove that value was delivered
  • Fill every Sprint to a target number

Story points are relative estimates created in a team-specific context. A higher number does not automatically indicate greater productivity, and comparing velocity across teams is misleading.

A velocity change should create questions.

For example:

  • Did the Definition of Done change?
  • Was team availability different?
  • Were items larger?
  • Did urgent work interrupt the Sprint?
  • Was unfinished work carried over?
  • Did the team spend more time reducing risk?

The detailed estimation context is explored in implementing story points without turning them into productivity measures.

Throughput

Throughput is the number of work items completed during a period.

For example:

The team completed 18 items during the last four weeks.

Throughput is simple, but it still requires context.

It becomes misleading when the work items being counted differ significantly in type or size.

A team could apparently double throughput by splitting every item more narrowly without delivering more value.

Throughput is most useful when:

  • Items are reasonably comparable
  • Work types are separated where appropriate
  • Trends are inspected over time
  • It is combined with quality and outcome measures
  • It supports probabilistic forecasting

The Kanban Guide defines throughput as the exact number of work items finished per unit of time.

Lead time

Lead time measures how long somebody waits for an outcome.

A product-level definition may begin when a customer need or request is accepted and end when the change is available to the customer.

Lead time therefore often includes:

  • Waiting before work starts
  • Analysis
  • Development
  • Review
  • Testing
  • Deployment
  • Other queues and delays

It is useful for questions such as:

  • How long do customers wait?
  • Is the backlog queue growing?
  • Are requests spending more time waiting than being worked on?
  • Did a process change shorten delivery from end to end?

Microsoft’s Azure DevOps guidance distinguishes lead time from cycle time and allows teams to configure the boundaries according to their workflow.

Always display the start and finish points beside the result.

Worked example — the same item, three boundaries

Request accepted on day 6. Work started on day 18. Development complete on day 38. Released to customers on day 40.

Request accepted → released: 40 − 6 = 34 days. This is what the customer waited.

Request accepted → development complete: 38 − 6 = 32 days.

Work started → finished (cycle time): 38 − 18 = 20 days.

One item, three defensible numbers ranging from 20 to 34 days. A team reporting the smallest one is not faster — it is measuring less of the wait. Twelve of the 34 days were queueing before anyone touched the work.

Cycle time

Cycle time measures elapsed time from when a work item starts until it finishes.

Unlike lead time, it normally excludes the period before work begins.

The current Kanban Guide defines cycle time as the elapsed time between an item’s agreed started and finished points.

Cycle time helps teams inspect:

  • How quickly active work is completed
  • Whether work is becoming stuck
  • Variation between work types
  • The effect of work-in-progress limits
  • Whether smaller items move more predictably
  • Whether reviews or testing create queues

Do not focus only on the average.

An average of six days may hide a pattern where most items complete in three days while several take more than twenty.

Use the distribution and percentiles to understand variation.

A service-level expectation might state:

85% of standard items are completed within eight days of starting.

This is a forecast based on historical evidence, not a guarantee. The Kanban Guide describes an SLE using an elapsed-time period and an associated probability.

Work item age

Cycle time only becomes available after an item is finished.

Work item age helps the team inspect active work before it is too late.

It measures how long an unfinished item has existed since its agreed started point.

Useful questions include:

  • Which active item is oldest?
  • Has it exceeded the normal cycle-time range?
  • Is it blocked?
  • Is anybody still working on it?
  • Can the team swarm to complete it?
  • Should it be split?
  • Has its priority changed after work began?

An ageing item is a warning signal, not proof that somebody has failed.

Work in progress

Work in progress, or WIP, is the number of items started but not finished.

High WIP often creates:

  • Longer waiting
  • More context switching
  • Slower feedback
  • More ageing work
  • More unfinished items
  • Less predictable delivery

A WIP limit does not exist to keep people idle.

It encourages the team to finish existing work before pulling in more.

The useful decision is not:

How do we keep every person busy?

It is:

How do we help valuable work reach Done sooner?

Cumulative flow diagrams

A cumulative flow diagram shows how the number of work items in each workflow state changes over time.

Each coloured band represents a state such as:

  • Ready
  • In progress
  • Review
  • Testing
  • Done

The diagram helps teams inspect:

  • Work in progress
  • Arrival and completion patterns
  • Growing queues
  • Bottlenecks
  • Approximate lead or cycle time
  • Whether the system is becoming more stable

If one band continually becomes wider, work is entering that state faster than it leaves.

For example, a widening Testing band may indicate:

  • Testing capacity is constrained
  • Work reaches testing too late
  • Items are too large
  • Environments are unstable
  • Quality problems create repeated rework

A cumulative flow diagram identifies where to investigate. It does not explain the cause by itself. Microsoft and Atlassian both describe CFDs as views of work-item states over time that can expose WIP and flow behaviour.

Blocked time

Blocked time measures how long work is unable to progress because of an impediment.

Examples include:

  • Waiting for an external decision
  • Environment unavailability
  • Supplier dependency
  • Missing access
  • Unresolved technical dependency
  • Required specialist unavailable

Total blocked time can help identify recurring system constraints.

However, a blocked label should have an explicit meaning.

Do not use it for:

  • Ordinary waiting inside the workflow
  • Work nobody is actively progressing
  • Low priority
  • General complexity

Useful decisions include:

  • Escalating a repeated dependency
  • Creating an alternative environment
  • Reducing reliance on one specialist
  • Changing the delivery sequence
  • Splitting work around a constraint

Predictability

Predictability is not the same as delivering the same number of points every Sprint.

A predictable system allows the team to make forecasts with a known level of uncertainty.

Useful measures include:

  • Percentage of items completed within the service-level expectation
  • Cycle-time percentiles
  • Throughput ranges
  • Forecast accuracy
  • Sprint Goal success
  • Frequency of unplanned interruption

For example:

Based on the last 30 similar items, 85% completed within eight days.

This is generally more informative than:

The average cycle time is six days.

The first statement includes a probability and makes uncertainty visible.

Bug bounce-back and reopen rates

“Bug bounce-back rate” is not a universally standardised metric.

Teams commonly use the phrase to describe defects or work items that are sent for validation and then returned to development because the problem remains unresolved or the change fails validation.

Define the exact workflow before using it.

A possible formula is:

Bounce-back rate = items returned from validation ÷ items entering validation × 100

A related measure is bug reopen rate:

Reopen rate = closed bugs reopened ÷ bugs closed × 100

Use reason categories such as:

  • Original defect not fixed
  • Regression introduced
  • Acceptance criteria misunderstood
  • Environment or data issue
  • Unable to reproduce
  • Fix incomplete
  • Different defect discovered

A rising rate may indicate:

  • Weak shared understanding
  • Inadequate testing before handoff
  • Poor reproduction information
  • Fragile areas of the product
  • Pressure to close defects too early

Do not use bounce-back rates to rank individual Developers or testers.

The metric describes rework in the delivery system.

Research into reopened defects identifies several contributing causes, including patch, integration, documentation and assessment problems, reinforcing the need to inspect reasons rather than only the total.

Escaped defects and change failure

A defect escape is a problem discovered after the stage where the team intended to detect it.

The most important escape is often a defect discovered by customers after release.

Useful quality measures include:

  • Customer-reported defects
  • Escaped defects by severity
  • Defects per release
  • Time to detect
  • Time to restore service
  • Repeat incidents
  • Change failure rate
  • Deployment rework rate

DORA defines change failure rate as the proportion of deployments requiring immediate intervention and deployment rework rate as unplanned deployment work caused by a production incident.

Bug counts need a denominator.

Ten defects from two releases mean something different from ten defects across one hundred releases.

Quality measures should also be interpreted alongside the Definition of Done — the shared quality boundary that determines what can genuinely be called complete.

Measure trends, not isolated numbers

A single result rarely tells the full story.

Inspect:

  • Direction over time
  • Variation
  • Distribution
  • Work type
  • Severity
  • Changes to workflow
  • Changes to team capacity
  • Changes to the Definition of Done
  • External events
  • Customer outcomes

Avoid reacting to every small movement.

Normal variation does not always require intervention.

Look for sustained patterns, unusual outliers and changes with a plausible connection to the system.

A metric should trigger investigation.

The metric is not the decision.

It is evidence used in the decision.

Avoid metric misuse

Metrics become harmful when they are used to:

  • Rank teams
  • Reward individuals
  • Punish honest reporting
  • Demand constant improvement
  • Compare unlike work
  • Hide quality work
  • Replace customer evidence
  • Support a decision already made
  • Create targets without understanding behaviour

When a metric becomes a target, people naturally adapt to the target.

They may:

  • Inflate estimates
  • Split items unnaturally
  • Delay bug reporting
  • Close defects prematurely
  • Avoid difficult work
  • Reduce testing
  • Change workflow states
  • Optimise the number while weakening the product

Measures should help teams learn, not force them to defend themselves.

A practical measurement approach

1. Start with the decision

Examples:

  • Should we reduce WIP?
  • Is testing becoming a bottleneck?
  • Can we forecast delivery more reliably?
  • Did the new review process reduce waiting?
  • Is quality improving after release?

2. Choose the smallest useful set of measures

Use only the evidence needed to understand the decision.

3. Define the boundaries

Document work types, start points, finish points and exclusions.

4. Establish a baseline

Understand the current pattern before expecting change.

5. Make one deliberate change

For example, reduce WIP or involve testing earlier.

6. Inspect the trend

Combine quantitative evidence with the team’s experience.

7. Decide what happens next

Adopt, adapt or stop the change.

This connects naturally to turning retrospective feedback into owned and measurable improvement experiments.

A balanced starter set

A team does not need every metric immediately.

A practical starting set is:

  • One customer or product outcome
  • Lead time
  • Cycle time
  • Work item age
  • WIP
  • Throughput
  • One quality measure
  • Sprint Goal success where Scrum is used

Add another measure only when it supports a real decision.

Conclusion

Metrics should increase transparency, not surveillance.

Velocity can support a team’s forecast.

Lead time shows how long somebody waits.

Cycle time shows how long active work takes.

Work item age exposes risk before work finishes.

WIP shows how much has been started.

Throughput shows how much is completed.

A cumulative flow diagram shows where work accumulates.

Quality measures reveal rework, escape and instability.

Outcome measures show whether the product actually improved anything.

None of them is sufficient alone.

Measure the system, understand the context and use the evidence to decide what to change next.
